The design decisions aimed to make the visualization both clear and compelling, while navigating the line between persuasion and distortion. Below are key choices and their justifications:

  • Grouped bar chart — Score: 2 (earnest)
    Chosen for side-by-side comparison of China and U.S. across years. Clear and direct. Line charts were too cluttered.
  • Symlog transformation — Score: -1 (somewhat deceptive)
    Used to handle extreme values and make trends readable. But it compresses the visual impact of larger swings.

Final Reflection

Throughout the design process, selecting the right dataset to complement the emissions data was more challenging than expected. I experimented with several options — GDP, GDP per capita, and other economic indicators — before ultimately deciding that absolute GDP change told the clearest story. My primary goal was to present an alternative perspective on China's emissions behavior, and finding a pairing that could persuasively support that narrative took time. I opted to use percentage changes instead of raw values to avoid relying on a dual-axis chart, which can often feel cluttered or misleading. That said, I acknowledge that some of the transformations I used — like a symmetric log scale and moving averages — stretch the boundaries of ethical visualization. While these techniques can help reveal trends, I was also aware that adjusting parameters like the rolling mean window or symlog threshold could amplify the effect I wanted to emphasize. This raises a fundamental ethical question: when does adjusting data presentation shift from insightful to manipulative? In my view, it becomes unethical when the transformation isn’t necessary for comprehension but is instead used to push a narrative. Looking back, I now define ethical analysis and visualization as grounded in transparency and intent. If a transformation aids understanding without distorting the truth, it’s likely ethical. But if it serves primarily to exaggerate or conceal aspects of the data, it crosses into misleading territory. Persuasion in data storytelling is not inherently unethical — it’s about where you draw the line. For me, that line is clear: transformations must serve clarity, not bias.
